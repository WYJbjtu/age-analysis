## Main explanation

Point first to the pooled scatter, then the country panels.

<!-- spoken:start -->
We connected the population and industry data for all 98 regions: 50 U.S. states and Washington, D.C., and 47 Japanese prefectures. We checked the original official sources, and no region is missing from either our 2022 analysis or our 2019 comparison. Each region has equal weight.

The horizontal axis is the percentage of residents aged 65 or older. The vertical axis is manufacturing value added as a percentage of nominal regional GDP.

Looking only at the pooled panel, we might conclude that older regions tend to have larger manufacturing shares. Pearson correlation is 0.644. But within the United States it is just 0.003, and within Japan it is 0.029. Both are close to zero. Rank correlations are also weak, and the 2019 comparison shows the same broad contrast.

Why does pooling change the picture so much? Japan's prefectures generally have both older populations and higher manufacturing shares than U.S. states. Our covariance calculation separates differences between country means from variation within countries. About 99.1 percent of pooled covariance is between countries. This describes how the overall pattern is assembled; it does not explain its causes.

Pearson does not reverse direction. Rank correlations have a nominal sign reversal, but their within-country values are weak. We therefore do not claim a robust Simpson's paradox. Removing Washington, D.C., changes the U.S. correlation to minus 0.103. Population weighting also changes the country results, so near-zero equal-region correlations do not prove that no relationship exists.

There are practical limits. U.S. GDP uses calendar years, while Japan uses fiscal years. The countries' regional aging ranges do not overlap. Our similar-age examples are therefore within-country comparisons. Labor and migration provide context, but cannot establish a mechanism. These regional associations also cannot tell us how aging affects an individual person or firm.
<!-- spoken:end -->
